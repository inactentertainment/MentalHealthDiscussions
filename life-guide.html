Workplace Rights

Accommodation is about function.

Under the ADA, qualifying mental-health conditions can support reasonable accommodations. FMLA can separately provide job-protected leave for eligible workers of covered employers when a serious mental-health condition meets the law's requirements.

Possible accommodations

  • Modified schedules or breaks
  • Time for treatment appointments
  • Quiet or reduced-distraction workspace
  • Written instructions or changes in supervisory communication
  • Telework in appropriate jobs
  • Other individualized changes tied to functional limitations

What you may need to say

You generally do not need magic words. A request can communicate that you need a change at work because of a medical condition. The employer may ask for reasonable documentation when the disability or need is not obvious.

What accommodation does not automatically do

The ADA does not require an employer to remove essential job functions or excuse all performance problems. The goal is an effective, reasonable change that helps the worker perform the job.

FMLA is different from ADA accommodation. Eligible employees may use FMLA leave for their own qualifying serious mental-health condition or, in some circumstances, to care for a spouse, child, or parent with a qualifying serious health condition. Coverage and eligibility rules matter.

School Supports

K–12 and college are not the same system.

Section 504, Title II, IDEA, and postsecondary disability-service procedures can overlap, but the responsibilities and process change as students move from K–12 into college.

K–12

  • Section 504 can protect students whose physical or mental impairment substantially limits a major life activity.
  • IDEA can provide special education and related services when a student meets an IDEA disability category and needs special education because of it.
  • Mental-health conditions may affect attendance, concentration, behavior, testing, stamina, or access to instruction.
  • Families can ask the school how to request an evaluation under Section 504 and/or IDEA.

College / university

  • Students generally need to identify their disability-related need and request academic adjustments or auxiliary aids through the institution's process.
  • Possible modifications can include reduced-distraction testing, extended time, reduced course load, make-up work, disability-related absences, or medical leave when appropriate.
  • Accommodations are individualized; a diagnosis alone does not dictate one standard package.
Disclosure Decision

How much do you actually need to tell?

Disclosure can be useful when it is needed to request support, but more information is not always better. Think in terms of purpose, recipient, and minimum necessary detail.

At work

For an accommodation request, focus on the medical-condition-related limitation and the workplace change you need. Medical information obtained through the accommodation process is generally subject to ADA confidentiality rules.

At school

Use the school's disability/504/special-education process rather than repeatedly explaining your condition to every teacher or professor when a formal accommodation process is available.

With coworkers or classmates

You may choose to share more personally, but that is different from what an employer, school, or disability office needs to evaluate an accommodation request.

Return After Crisis

Re-entry should be a plan, not a cliff.

After hospitalization, intensive treatment, or a major episode, the transition back to work or school can be as important as the discharge itself.

1. Clarify readinessWhat can you realistically do now? What still needs treatment, rest, or monitoring?
2. Identify barriersSchedule, workload, concentration, sleep, transportation, medication effects, social stress.
3. Arrange supportsAccommodation, leave, reduced load, follow-up appointments, check-ins, case management.
4. Set a relapse planWarning signs, whom to contact, what happens if functioning drops again, and when urgent help is needed.
Daily Life

Functioning is part of mental health.

Sometimes the most useful next step is not another label—it is reducing the number of things that can fall apart at once.

Make the day visible

Use one calendar or task list for medication, appointments, transportation, work/school deadlines, meals, bills, and sleep instead of relying on memory when symptoms are high.

Simplify decisions

Reduce unnecessary choices during difficult periods: repeat meals, set medication reminders, automate bills where safe, prepare transportation in advance, and break tasks into smaller steps.

Notice the functional warning signs

Missed work or class, unopened mail, unpaid bills, poor hygiene, sleep reversal, isolation, unsafe driving, or inability to keep appointments can be important changes to discuss with a treatment team.
