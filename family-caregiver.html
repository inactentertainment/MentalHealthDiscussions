Special Focus

Treatment refusal intelligence.

Refusal can come from many places: side effects, previous bad experiences, cost, fear, stigma, disagreement about diagnosis, substance use, cognitive problems, or lack of awareness of illness. The response should match the situation.

Autonomy + safety both matterAn adult who has decision-making capacity often has substantial authority over treatment decisions. Emergency, incapacity, guardianship, involuntary evaluation, and court-ordered treatment rules vary by state. MHD does not tell families they can force care simply because they disagree with a loved one.

What situation is closest?

Choose the closest starting point. The guidance is educational, not a legal determination.

Communication Intelligence

Reduce friction before you increase pressure.

When someone strongly disagrees with the diagnosis or treatment plan, repeated arguments about who is right can damage trust. Focus on observable problems, shared goals, and choices the person can still make.

Start with what they want.

Ask what is bothering them most: sleep, fear, work, money, side effects, housing, conflict, feeling watched, or simply wanting everyone to stop pressuring them. A shared problem can become an entry point to care.

Describe; do not prosecute.

Use concrete observations: “You have slept about two hours a night for four nights,” rather than “You are obviously manic.” Facts are often easier to discuss than labels.

Reflect before persuading.

Show that you heard the person’s concern even when you disagree. Understanding a belief is not the same as agreeing that it is accurate.

Offer meaningful choices.

When possible, offer options: which provider, telehealth or in person, morning or afternoon, whether a supporter attends, or which problem gets discussed first.

Do not secretly alter treatment.

Do not hide medication, change doses, or substitute supplements without appropriate clinical guidance. Bring adherence problems and side effects to the treatment team.

Know when conversation is no longer enough.

Immediate danger, major medical instability, severe confusion, inability to care for basic needs, or rapidly escalating behavior can require urgent professional assessment.

About anosognosia: some people with schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, neurological illness, or other serious conditions can have impaired awareness of illness. It is different from simply being stubborn or making an ordinary informed choice. Lack of insight can vary over time.

Family + HIPAA

“They can’t tell me” does not mean “I can’t tell them.”

HIPAA often limits what a provider can disclose to a family member without the patient’s permission. It does not prevent a provider from listening to information a family member or caregiver wants to share.

Family can give information.

HHS states that HIPAA does not prevent health-care providers from listening to family members or caregivers who have concerns about an adult patient’s mental health.

What providers can disclose depends on consent and circumstances.

If an adult with capacity objects to disclosure, the provider generally must respect that choice, subject to limited exceptions such as serious and imminent threats and other applicable law.

If the patient allows involvement, relevant information can be shared.

When the patient agrees or does not object, HIPAA permits providers to share information directly relevant to the family member’s involvement in care.

Incapacity can change the analysis.

If a patient is not present or is incapacitated, a provider may use professional judgment to share relevant information with people involved in care when it is in the patient’s best interests.

State law can be stricter.

Mental-health confidentiality, minors, guardianship, involuntary treatment, and substance-use records can involve additional laws. HIPAA is not the only rule.

Ask for a release early.

When the person is willing, ask the treatment team how to document permission for family involvement before a crisis. Permission can often be limited to specific people or information.

Crisis + Transitions

What families should ask before the next handoff.

Hospital discharge, emergency evaluation, first-episode psychosis care, and major medication changes are high-information moments. Write down what the team says.

Before discharge or leaving urgent care

  • What diagnosis or working explanation is being considered?
  • What medications changed, and what side effects or warning signs require a call?
  • What is the follow-up appointment date, and who is responsible for scheduling it?
  • What should happen if symptoms return before that appointment?
  • Are there restrictions involving driving, work, alcohol, substances, sleep, or activities?

For first-episode psychosis

  • Ask whether a Coordinated Specialty Care program is available.
  • Ask about family education/support, medication management, therapy, supported work/school, and case management.
  • Ask how shared decision-making will work and what the person’s goals are.

If treatment was refused

  • Ask what concern drove the refusal: side effects, disagreement, fear, logistics, previous trauma, cost, or lack of perceived need.
  • Ask what lower-friction option might still be acceptable.
  • Ask what observable changes should trigger urgent reassessment.

For the family

  • Know who to call during office hours and after hours.
  • Keep an accurate medication list and a short timeline of major changes.
  • Clarify what information the person agrees can be shared with family.
  • Make a plan for transportation, housing, food, sleep, appointments, and other immediate practical needs.
Family Support

Helping does not mean controlling everything.

Caregivers need boundaries, information, rest, and support of their own. A family system that is exhausted or constantly fighting has less capacity to help.

Support autonomy.

Whenever safety allows, involve the person in decisions and focus on goals that matter to them. NIMH’s early-psychosis guidance emphasizes shared decision-making and person-centered recovery.

Set limits on what you can provide.

You can care about someone and still set boundaries around money, housing rules, aggression, substance use in the home, transportation, or what you are able to manage.

Get support for the caregiver.

Family education, peer groups, counseling, and caregiver support can reduce isolation and help families communicate more effectively.
