What happens next when a mental-health crisis reaches the system?

Understand the path from crisis call or emergency department to evaluation, voluntary or involuntary hospitalization, patient rights, family participation, discharge planning, advance directives, and the legal questions that vary by state.

Emergency Evaluation

What usually happens.

The exact process depends on the hospital, state law, medical needs, available beds, and whether the person agrees to treatment. This is a general U.S. roadmap, not a guarantee of sequence or timing.

Step 1

Someone asks for help.

The person, family, clinician, 988 counselor, emergency service, law enforcement, or another route may connect the person to crisis services or an emergency department.

Step 2

Safety + medical screening.

Teams look for immediate danger, overdose or intoxication, delirium, injury, medication effects, neurological or other medical causes, and urgent psychiatric symptoms.

Step 3

Psychiatric assessment.

Clinicians ask about symptoms, recent behavior, sleep, substances, medications, history, supports, functioning, and risk. Family observations can be useful.

Step 4

Disposition decision.

Possible next steps include discharge with a safety/follow-up plan, crisis stabilization, voluntary inpatient care, or an involuntary process if legal criteria are met.

Step 5

Transition.

If admitted, treatment and legal status continue to be reviewed. If discharged, the most important questions become medications, follow-up, warning signs, transportation, housing, and who to call if symptoms return.

What 988 does988 is a free U.S. crisis line. A counselor first asks about safety, listens, provides support, and shares resources. 988 is not automatically the same thing as police or hospitalization.
Hospital Navigation

Voluntary, involuntary, and everything in between.

The labels matter because they affect consent, leaving the hospital, hearings, legal representation, and what standards the facility must meet.

Family Preparation

What to bring or have ready.

In a crisis, families often remember the story but not the details the clinical team needs. A one-page factual packet can save time.

Medication + medical information

  • Current prescription medications, dose, schedule, and last known dose
  • OTC medicines, vitamins, herbs, and supplements
  • Medication allergies and serious past reactions
  • Major medical or neurological conditions
  • Recent medication starts, stops, dose changes, missed doses, or long-acting injections
  • Recent alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, sedatives, or other substance use if known

Behavior + timeline

  • What changed from baseline and approximately when
  • Sleep pattern, eating, hygiene, work/school, driving, spending, isolation, speech, activity, suspiciousness, confusion, or unusual perceptions
  • Specific safety concerns rather than only labels such as “psychotic” or “manic”
  • Recent hospitalization, emergency visits, or major stressors

Documents + contacts

  • Photo ID and insurance information if available
  • Advance directive / psychiatric advance directive
  • Health-care agent or emergency contact information
  • Current psychiatrist, therapist, primary-care clinician, pharmacy, and case manager
  • Legal guardian documentation if one actually exists

Practical preparation

  • Phone charger, glasses/hearing aids, essential mobility or communication items if the facility allows them
  • Do not assume the hospital will permit outside medications, cords, valuables, tobacco/vapes, or all personal items
  • Ask the facility what belongings are allowed before bringing a bag
Family information can still matter even when the hospital cannot tell you much. HIPAA does not stop a provider from listening to concerns a family member or caregiver wants to share. What the hospital can disclose back depends on permission, capacity, involvement in care, threat exceptions, and other law.
State Legal Layer

Maryland starter module.

Mental-health commitment law is state-specific. This is the first state module in the MHD legal layer and is based on Maryland law in effect October 5, 2026.

Maryland: emergency evaluation + hospital admission

Current-law educational summary. Always verify current forms and law before acting.

Law checked: October 5, 2026

Emergency Petition (EP)

An emergency evaluation petition is used to have a person taken for emergency examination when the legal standard is met. Maryland Courts states that the process concerns a person believed to have a mental disorder and to present danger to life or safety.

An EP may be initiated by certain clinicians, law enforcement based on observation, or another interested person. A court-filed petition must describe behavior, statements, and other supporting facts.

Maryland Courts: Emergency Evaluations

Current Maryland “danger” definition

Chapter 177 took effect October 1, 2026 and added a statutory definition used for emergency evaluation and involuntary admission. The definition focuses on a substantial risk resulting from a mental disorder and considers the person’s current condition and, when available, recent personal, medical, and psychiatric history.

The law includes serious bodily-harm risk and severe inability to meet basic needs creating a substantial risk of serious bodily harm, serious illness, or death in the near future. Read the statute itself for the full current definition.

Maryland Chapter 177 (2026)

After an EP reaches the emergency facility

Maryland Courts says a physician must evaluate the person within 6 hours after arrival to determine whether involuntary-admission requirements are met. The emergency facility generally may not keep an emergency evaluee more than 30 hours under this process.

If involuntary criteria are met and the person will not agree to voluntary admission, the physician takes steps toward involuntary admission.

Maryland Courts process

Maryland involuntary-admission standard

Health-General §10-617 requires a mental disorder, need for inpatient care/treatment, danger to life or safety, inability or unwillingness to be admitted voluntarily, and no available less restrictive intervention consistent with welfare and safety.

§10-617

Hearing after involuntary admission

Maryland law provides for an involuntary-admission hearing within 10 days of initial confinement, subject to a limited postponement for good cause. The hearing officer must order release unless the statutory elements are proven by clear and convincing evidence.

Parent, guardian, or next of kin must receive notice of the hearing and may testify.

§10-632

Voluntary admission in Maryland

Under §10-609, a person age 16 or older may seek voluntary admission if the statutory requirements are met, including understanding the request, continuous assent, and ability to ask for release.

Maryland also distinguishes informal and formal voluntary admission. Release rules differ, and a facility may begin an involuntary process if the legal criteria are met.

§10-609

Leaving voluntary inpatient care

Maryland §10-803 says an informal voluntary patient generally may leave between 9 a.m. and 4 p.m. unless status changes to involuntary. A formal voluntary patient generally may not be held more than 3 days after requesting release unless status changes to involuntary.

§10-803

Rights + medication

Maryland’s psychiatric-facility rights materials address respectful care, safe conditions, understandable information, privacy, advance directives, grievances, and medication rights. Some rights have exceptions in emergencies or under involuntary-treatment procedures.

Maryland Patient Bill of Rights

Mental-health advance directive

Maryland allows a mental-health advance directive to state treatment preferences and identify people authorized to make decisions or receive information if the person later cannot make or communicate decisions.

It can include preferred hospitals/providers, medication preferences, allergies/adverse reactions, visitors, and other treatment instructions.

Maryland Mental Health Advance Directives

Legal representation / Public Defender

Maryland law requires notice to the Mental Health Division in the Office of the Public Defender after an emergency facility completes an application for involuntary admission. The hospital or rights staff can explain representation and hearing procedures.

§10-625
Rights + Decision-Making

Terms that families often confuse.

Diagnosis, hospitalization, incapacity, guardianship, and involuntary treatment are not interchangeable legal concepts.

Capacity

Decision-making capacity is a clinical and legal concept tied to a particular decision and circumstance. A person can have a serious diagnosis and still retain decision-making ability. Capacity can also change over time.

Involuntary hospitalization

Being hospitalized involuntarily does not automatically mean a person has lost every legal right or is globally incapable of making all decisions. Specific treatment and rights questions depend on governing law and circumstances.

Guardianship

Guardianship is a separate court process. Family relationship alone does not make someone the legal guardian of another adult. The scope of a guardian’s authority depends on the court order and state law.

Advance directive

A psychiatric or mental-health advance directive lets a person state preferences while they have decision-making ability for a future period when they may not be able to communicate or decide.

HIPAA

HIPAA governs certain health-information disclosures. It does not create a blanket rule that family can never receive information, and it does not prevent providers from listening to relevant information from family.

Less restrictive alternative

Many involuntary-treatment systems require consideration of whether a safer, less restrictive option can meet the person’s needs before inpatient commitment is justified.

Discharge Navigation

Do not leave with only “follow up outpatient.”

A useful discharge plan turns a hospital stay into a next step. Families and patients should ask concrete questions before the transition whenever possible.

Medication plan

  • What was started, stopped, or changed?
  • What is each medication for?
  • When is the next dose?
  • What side effects or warning signs require a call or urgent care?
  • Where will refills come from?

Follow-up

  • Exact appointment date, time, place, and provider
  • Who calls if the appointment falls through?
  • Is therapy, psychiatry, primary care, case management, or a specialty program needed?
  • For early psychosis, is Coordinated Specialty Care available?

Safety + relapse plan

  • What warning signs suggest the condition is returning?
  • Who should be contacted first?
  • When should the person return to the emergency department?
  • What does the person want family/supporters to do?

Daily-life transition

  • Transportation and housing
  • Food, sleep, and medication access
  • Work/school documentation or leave needs
  • Driving or activity restrictions
  • Substance-use recommendations

Information + permissions

  • Who is authorized to receive updates?
  • Is a release-of-information form needed?
  • Does an advance directive need to be created or updated?
  • Who gets the discharge summary and medication list?

Questions after an involuntary stay

  • What legal status was used and when did it change?
  • Were there hearings or orders that continue after discharge?
  • Are there any state-specific restrictions or follow-up obligations?
  • Who can explain appeal, grievance, or rights procedures?
Family notification is not all-or-nothing. HHS explains that hospitals may notify or share certain relevant information with family/caregivers in multiple circumstances, including when the patient agrees or does not object, when an incapacitated patient’s best interests support sharing, or when necessary to lessen a serious and imminent threat.
Before a Future Crisis

Prepare while choices are easier.

The best time to discuss preferences is often before the next emergency.

Create an advance directive.

Document preferred hospitals, decision-makers, treatment preferences, allergies, people who may receive information, and other instructions allowed by state law.

Build a one-page crisis packet.

Medication list, diagnoses, medical conditions, providers, emergency contacts, insurance, advance directive, baseline functioning, and what has helped in past crises.

Clarify family permissions.

Ask the provider how to complete a release of information so family involvement is not being negotiated for the first time during a crisis.
