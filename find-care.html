How do I actually get help?

Move from “something is wrong” to a concrete next step. Choose the kind of help you need, understand levels of care and provider credentials, find official treatment locators, prepare insurance questions, and build the call or message that gets the process started.

Start with urgency, not a diagnosis. If there is immediate danger, a suspected overdose, severe medical instability, or someone cannot stay safe, use emergency/crisis care rather than waiting for a routine outpatient appointment.

Levels of Care

More support does not always mean a hospital.

Care intensity should match clinical need, safety, functioning, and what can realistically be supported in the community.

Routine / lower intensity

Standard outpatient

Periodic therapy, medication management, primary care, or both while living normally in the community.

  • Often weekly, biweekly, monthly, or as needed
  • Best when safety and daily functioning can be managed between visits
Moderate intensity

IOP

Intensive Outpatient Program: several hours of structured treatment on multiple days each week without overnight stay.

  • More structure than ordinary outpatient care
  • Can support step-up or step-down care
Higher daytime intensity

PHP

Partial Hospitalization Program: highly structured day treatment, commonly several hours per day on multiple days each week.

  • No overnight hospital stay
  • Often used after inpatient care or when outpatient care is not enough
24-hour / highest acute intensity

Inpatient hospital

24-hour hospital-based treatment for serious symptoms requiring intensive safety, medical, or psychiatric monitoring.

  • Usually short-term acute stabilization
  • Discharge planning should connect to follow-up care
Specialty early intervention

Coordinated Specialty Care

Team-based treatment for first-episode psychosis or recent serious mental illness, often combining medication, therapy, family support, case management, peer support, and work/school services.

Substance-use specialty

SUD treatment

Can range from outpatient counseling and medication treatment to IOP, residential, opioid treatment programs, withdrawal management, or hospital care.

Community response

Mobile crisis / crisis stabilization

Community-based crisis assessment and stabilization may be available without automatically requiring inpatient hospitalization.

Primary entry point

Primary care

A primary care clinician can screen for common mental-health concerns, consider medical causes, begin some treatments, and refer to specialty care.

Insurance & Access Checklist

Before the first appointment gets expensive.

Ask these questions before assuming a provider, program, or medication is covered.

Call the plan or check the portal

If you do not have insurance

  • Ask HRSA health centers and local community mental-health agencies about sliding fees or uninsured access.
  • Use FindTreatment.gov and ask programs specifically about self-pay, grants, state funding, or reduced-fee care.
  • Ask teaching clinics or university training programs whether supervised reduced-fee therapy is available.
  • Check Medicaid eligibility in your state if income or disability circumstances may qualify.
  • For medications, ask the prescriber or pharmacist about lower-cost generics and legitimate assistance options.
When Nobody Is Accepting Patients

Do not stop after one “no.”

Access failures are common enough that the search itself needs a plan.

1. Ask for the wait list.Ask whether cancellations are offered and how often the list is reviewed.
2. Widen the route.Try primary care, telehealth, community clinics, hospital outpatient programs, and nearby counties.
3. Ask the insurer to help.Request current in-network names and document when listed providers are not actually available.
4. Ask for interim care.Primary care, bridge medication visits, group therapy, peer support, or short-term programs may help while waiting.
5. Escalate if symptoms escalate.If safety or functioning deteriorates, move to crisis, urgent, IOP/PHP, or emergency assessment as appropriate.

Questions Before You Commit

“Accepting patients” is only the beginning.

A good match includes competence, logistics, cost, communication, and a treatment approach that makes sense for the problem.

Experience

  • How much experience do you have with this problem?
  • What treatment approaches do you usually use?
  • How do you measure whether treatment is helping?

Logistics

  • How soon is the first appointment?
  • What happens between visits if symptoms worsen?
  • Do you offer telehealth? What is the cancellation policy?

Cost & coverage

  • Are you in-network for my exact plan?
  • What will I owe per visit?
  • Are labs, testing, facility fees, or other services billed separately?
