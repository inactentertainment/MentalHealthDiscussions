MHD Change, Advocacy & Systems Intelligence

Understand the system before trying to change it.

A nonpartisan guide to mental-health policy, legislation, regulation, budgets, insurance rules, public comments, testimony, and community advocacy. Start with the problem, identify who actually controls it, then make a clear evidence-based request.

Advocacy is not the same as diagnosis, treatment, or legal representation. MHD explains public processes and helps organize communication. Current bill status, regulations, deadlines, agency authority, and state law should always be verified at the official source before action.

Who Controls What?

Send the request to the right door.

Many advocacy efforts stall because the problem is sent to an official who does not actually control the decision.

Federal legislation

Congress

Federal statutes, appropriations, national programs, certain insurance and civil-rights laws, and oversight of federal agencies.

Federal administration

Agencies

Rules, guidance, grants, program administration, enforcement, data collection, and implementation of laws within agency authority.

State government

Legislature + governor + agencies

Licensing, Medicaid implementation, civil commitment, state hospitals, insurance regulation, crisis systems, budgets, schools, and many service rules vary by state.

Local government

County / city / boards

Local budgets, crisis contracts, public hospitals, law-enforcement partnerships, housing, schools, behavioral-health authorities, and community services may be controlled locally.

Policy Path

How change usually moves.

Different processes have different points where public input matters.

1. Define the problem.Who is affected? What is happening? What evidence shows the gap?
2. Identify authority.Statute, regulation, budget, agency practice, insurer, county, school, hospital, or court?
3. Find the live process.Bill, committee, budget hearing, proposed rule, agency docket, board meeting, complaint process.
4. Make one clear ask.Amend, fund, enforce, study, publish data, change a rule, expand access, or explain a decision.
5. Track the result.Status, amendments, final rule, appropriation, implementation date, evaluation, and unintended effects.

Bill & Rule Reader

Do not stop at the headline.

A proposal can sound simple while the operative language, funding, exceptions, enforcement, or implementation tells a different story.

Legislation

Read the status.

  • Bill number and Congress/session
  • Sponsor and cosponsors
  • Committee referral and hearings
  • Latest action and amendments
  • Companion bill, if any
  • Whether the text actually passed either chamber
Text

Read the operative language.

  • Who is covered?
  • What is required, allowed, prohibited, or funded?
  • Definitions and exceptions
  • Effective date
  • Funding authorization vs. actual appropriation
  • Reporting and enforcement
Rulemaking

Read the docket.

  • Proposed rule or notice
  • Agency authority
  • Comment deadline
  • Supporting studies and analyses
  • Public comments
  • Final rule and agency responses

Evidence Before Advocacy

Strong stories need strong facts.

Lived experience can explain impact. Research and official data help establish scope, cause, costs, and likely effects. They serve different purposes.

Official administrative dataGovernment reports, audits, claims data, budgets, wait times, licensing data, enforcement reports.
Systematic evidenceSystematic reviews, meta-analyses, major guidelines, well-designed trials or observational studies.
Local evidenceCounty data, hospital utilization, workforce shortages, school data, service closures, community needs assessments.
Lived experienceShows what implementation feels like, where processes break, and what outcomes matter to people.
Enter a claim and MHD will turn it into questions you can verify against the bill, rule, budget, source data, and implementation authority.
Public Comment

Comments are strongest when they are specific.

Federal notice-and-comment rulemaking generally allows the public to submit written input on proposed rules. Relevant, substantive comments become part of the rulemaking record.

Reference the proposal.

Identify the docket, section, definition, requirement, or question you are addressing rather than submitting only a general statement of support or opposition.

Explain why.

Provide evidence, operational experience, data, legal or clinical implications, implementation concerns, or a concrete alternative.

Protect privacy.

Public comments are generally publicly viewable. Do not include health, contact, or other identifying details you do not want disclosed.

System Reform Lens

Ask what changes after the announcement.

A policy is not finished when a law is signed, a grant is announced, or an agency publishes guidance.

Access

Did wait times, geographic access, language access, network adequacy, and affordability actually improve?

Quality

Are services evidence-based, person-centered, culturally responsive, and connected across crisis, hospital, outpatient, family, housing, school, and work systems?

Rights + safety

How are autonomy, due process, privacy, family involvement, discrimination, restraint/seclusion, and crisis safety addressed?

Accountability

Who reports outcomes? What data are public? What happens if implementation fails or produces unintended harm?
